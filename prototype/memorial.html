His Story

Elias James Hart was born in the spring of 1948 in Stonewall, Oklahoma, the third of five children, in a house his grandfather built by hand on red earth that had been in the family since the Territory days. He grew up working cattle before school and riding fence lines after it, and he used to say a man is measured by whether the gate still swings true after he's gone.

He married Martha Cole, his high-school sweetheart, in the summer of 1969. They raised three children on the same quarter-section, weathered drought years and good years shoulder to shoulder, and never once ate supper angry if Elias had anything to say about it. Folks in Stonewall knew him as the man who would stop for any stalled truck, any escaped calf, any neighbor who needed an extra set of hands — and stay until the work was done, not until it was easy.

"If you've got two hands and a neighbor, you've got enough." — Elias, most Sundays

His Work

  • Rancher — raised Angus cattle on the family land for over 45 years.
  • School bus driver — Route 4, Stonewall Public Schools, 22 years; never missed a morning a child was waiting.
  • Volunteer firefighter — Stonewall VFD, 1975–2003.
  • Deacon & song leader — First Baptist Church of Stonewall, where his baritone carried the hymns.

What He Loved

  • Playing fiddle on the porch at dusk — "Amazing Grace" was always the last tune of the night.
  • Hunting quail with a grandson on each side and a dog that minded better than either of them.
  • Cast-iron cooking, strong coffee, and pie he pretended not to want seconds of.
  • Telling the story of his grandfather — even if the details improved a little every year.

Heritage

Like many Oklahoma families, the Harts' story braids more than one thread. Family records trace the line back through Indian Territory, and Elias grew up on stories from both sides of that history — settler grit on one side, Native roots and a grandmother's hymns sung in two languages on the other.

This memorial service was designed to honor both lineages: where enrollment papers ask a family to choose one line, a family's memory doesn't have to. Stories, names, and languages from every branch belong on the same page.
